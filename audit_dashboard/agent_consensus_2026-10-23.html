2 · Buffy's point-in-time case study (2026-10-23): what makes a "consistently winning" fund?

71-fund NAV cache, decisions every Dec 2005–2022 using ONLY data ≤ that date, 12m holds, net of fees. 880 fund-years. Reproduce: python3 tools/mf_case_study_what_wins.py → reports/mf_case_study_2026-10-23.json. Limits: current ERs (no historical fee table), survivor-only universe, approximate historical minimums, monthly resolution.

ELI5: We lined up every fund's report card from last year and asked who wins next year. Nobody's past grades — top marks, steady marks, or "never fell much" — helped. The only thing that showed up was "how much stock-market ride you signed up for," which you get by simply owning the market. Over 5+ year spans, the only trait that helps is being cheap.

  • Every rule lost to plain SPY over 18 years (beat SPY only 3–7 of 18 single years). The gap = diversification drag (small/mid/intl sleeves lagging US large) + annual switching noise.
  • The "steady/predictable" picker was the worst (×2.30 vs ×4.62). ELI5: the smoothest road took the longest route.
  • Capital walls were the real story: until 2021–22 a $200–$2,000 account literally could not buy most funds (Fidelity $2,500 / Vanguard $3,000 minimums). At $200 the only passable picks were Schwab's $100-min index funds. At $2,000, Dodge & Cox ($1,000 min) enters and drags the tier to ×3.12. The 2022 zero-minimum revolution was the biggest small-account "edge" — access, not selection.
  • Would you have made money? Yes on every tier (2005–2023 was a strong bull era) — but you'd have left 15–90% on the table vs "do nothing but SPY."
