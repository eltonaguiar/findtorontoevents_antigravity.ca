🏆 What makes a mutual fund a WINNER? — walk-forward backtest (run 2026-10-22)

ELI5 — how we tested it: Imagine every December you hide all information from the future. You line the funds up using only what you could have known that day — how cheap they are, how smooth they rode the last 3 years, how they did last year — pick the top 3 by each rule, then look at what actually happened over the next 1, 3 and 5 years, compared to just buying a plain index fund (SPY in the US, XEQT.TO in Canada). You never get to peek: the score for December 2020 only uses data that existed on 2020-12-31. Every year's fee is subtracted before comparing. The years don't overlap, so one lucky decade can't be counted twice.

Technical: 71 funds (37 rated + 4 zero-fee ETFs + near-zero broad ETFs + 6 long-history active funds + 12 Canadian ETFs), rebalance at each year-end, 36-month trailing features computed strictly on rows ≤ t (unit-tested against future-data leakage), forward CAGR net of expense ratio, non-overlapping windows per horizon, paired t-stat + seeded 10,000-sample bootstrap CI on window excess returns. Reproduce: python3 tools/mf_walkforward_backtest.py --fetch && python3 tools/mf_walkforward_backtest.py --universe all — output data/mf_walkforward_results.json. 5/5 unit tests pass.

Benchmarks for reference: SPY mean forward ≈ 12.5%/10.7%/11.2% (1/3/5y) over these windows. No rule beat the plain index fund on average. The least-bad, most repeatable rule was simply buying the cheapest funds — which is exactly the split-half finding below.

Why cheap wins (the proof of the winner theory): split every fund's history in half, rank on the first half only, check the second half:

  • Expense ratio vs future returns: rank correlation −0.38 (cheaper → better next half).
  • Cheapest third of funds: +9.2%/yr in the second half vs +5.0%/yr for the dearest third — a 4-point gap, for free, on fees that differ by only ~0.05%.
  • Past smoothness (max drawdown) predicts almost nothing about future returns (ρ = −0.05) but does predict future pain (ρ = +0.58 for future drawdown).
  • Top second-half performers: SWLGX, SWLVX, SWMCX, FXAIX, SWPPX — all index funds at 0.01–0.04% fees.

The “secret lesser-known winner” hunt (style-adjusted, so we don't fool ourselves):

  • Raw top beaters of SPY: FSPGX +5.6%/yr, SWLGX +5.1%/yr — but both are large-cap growth index funds. Against a style-matched benchmark (Vanguard Growth, VIGAX) the edge vanishes: +0.2% and −0.1%/yr → growth beta, not skill.
  • FOCPX (Fidelity OTC, active, 0.78% fee) is the only fund whose edge SURVIVES style adjustment: +2.2%/yr vs VIGAX over 25 non-overlapping years (win 56%), and +4.0%/yr raw vs SPY stable in both 1994–2009 and 2010–2024. Caveats: worst years vs benchmark −23.5% (1999) and −22.2% (1996); concentrated-growth risk; one manager-era result. It is a CANDIDATE, not a proven winner.
  • Cheapest zero-fee ETFs (FZROX, FNILX): beat SPY 71–100% of 1-year windows — but they are index clones, so the “win” is just the fee difference.
  • Canada (vs XEQT.TO, 7×1y windows): no rule beats the all-in-one ETF; the cheap rule's −3.0% loss is mostly “the boring all-in-one beat the corner pieces”.

Bottom line: after fees and risk, our backtest agrees with the cross-agent consensus — no selection rule reliably beats a cheap index fund. The only repeatable edges are (1) paying less in fees, and (2) one style-adjusted active candidate (FOCPX) that still needs out-of-sample confirmation before anyone calls it a winner. 📜 Which funds actually "talk the talk"? — stated mandate vs delivered return (1994–2026 cache, ≥5y overlap)

ELI5: every fund document makes a promise — an index fund promises "whatever the index does, minus a tiny fee"; an active fund promises "more than the index, that's why you pay me". We tested each fund's promise against what it actually delivered, net of its fee, over every overlapping window we have (up to 30 years). An index fund that lands within ±0.15%/yr of its index delivers its documents. An active fund that trails its own style benchmark has no business charging an active fee.

🇨🇦 Canadian check (Zoo, independent): TDB902 (TD e-series S&P 500 index, CAD) — net CAGR 17.08%, Sharpe 1.09, MDD −27.5%; net + ~0.35% MER reconstructs the S&P 500 (CAD) benchmark ≈17.44% → tracking shortfall ≈ the fee exactly — the cleanest "talks the talk" profile in any study. Caveats: MER is assumed (verify at TD), NAV modeled via VFV.TO proxy, and the win is concentrated in US large-cap growth — a value regime would flip the ranking vs XEQT/TDB900.

Methodology — Antigravity §5.2 composite rating

cost 22%risk-adjusted 26%consistency 18% downside 16%capacity 10%manager 8%

All factor scores are category-relative percentiles (0–100) within the fund's style category, so a rating is "best-in-class" among peers, not absolute. Hard gates precede scoring: expense-ratio cap, AUM floor, NAV-history minimum, and 12b-1 load screening — failures are emitted as T3 Reject with a reason. Nearly-identical index clones are de-duplicated per category, keeping the highest-scoring representative.

Tiers: T1 Proven ≥ 80  ·  T2 Watch 65–80  ·  T3 Reject below 65 or failed gate. Metrics are computed from daily NAV (yfinance) over a 10-year lookback where available.

Sizing grade (A–D) is an illustrative capacity badge from AUM — how much capital the fund can plausibly absorb. Composite scores carry a freshness multiplier: no penalty within 7 days of last NAV, linear decay to 0.5× after 90 days stale. Tier-1/Tier-2 additionally require ≥ 100 months of NAV history.

Provenance: NAV series from yfinance; expense/load/AUM metadata manually curated. Resolution method: category-relative percentiles.
